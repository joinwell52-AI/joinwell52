# The Same Half-Score Can Hide Different Coverage in Scientific Workflows

Consider four queries. One system supplies outputs that pass the numeric filter for all four, but only two meet their targets. Another supplies just two outputs that pass the filter, and both meet their targets. Under the scoring equation examined here, both systems score 0.5. A selection decision based on that number alone cannot tell whether a change improved coverage or target attainment among the covered queries.

These are constructed cases from a completed internal experiment, not measurements of two deployed systems. The experiment examines the scoring definitions and reward description in Appendices C.2 and C.3 of ST-Bench v1. Our bounded proposition is that a scientific-workflow composite needs to be read alongside coverage, conditional target pass, task weights and metric validity: one score does not uniquely identify behavior.[1]

## Establish the denominator of an output

The paper calls certain outputs “realistic,” but the disclosed test is initially an operational numeric filter: defined metrics must be parseable and finite, at least one value must be positive, and no value may be the −1 sentinel. The term should not be read as a finding that a scientific conclusion is valid.[1, Appendix C.2]

The authors describe a task score as the fraction of queries passing that filter multiplied by the mean target pass rate conditional on those outputs. No qualifying outputs gives a zero score. In short notation, the task score is coverage times conditional pass. The paper sums these scores across 100 tasks for its headline; a task macro average would additionally divide that sum by the task count.[1]

Coverage asks how many queries enter the scoreable-output set. Conditional pass asks how often that selected set meets its stated targets. The latter is neither success over all queries nor an automatic measure of domain correctness. Different systems may cover different queries, so similar conditional rates do not establish equal scientific quality.

## Two half-scores and two averages

The internal experiment used constructed metrics and task contracts in Node v24.19.0 to evaluate boundary cases of the paper equations. It did not execute the upstream task packages, train agents or recompute the leaderboard. The following values are direct internal experimental evidence, with no independent third-party reproduction.[2]

| Constructed case | Queries | Outputs passing filter | Targets passed within that set | Coverage | Conditional pass | Task score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All scoreable, some meet target | 4 | 4 | 2 | 100% | 50% | 0.5 |
| Some scoreable, all meet target | 4 | 2 | 2 | 50% | 100% | 0.5 |

Every cell is from internal constructed inputs; source: the equal-headline cases in [2]. Both cases meet targets on the same number of total queries, but deliver different numbers of scoreable outputs. Their engineering behavior is not interchangeable: a downstream consumer requiring complete results may need to handle uncovered queries differently from outputs that miss a target.

A second case changes task sizes. Task one has a single query that passes its target; task two has nine queries that all miss. Both tasks have full numeric-output coverage. Their task scores are 1 and 0, giving a macro average of 0.5 and a paper-style score sum of 1. Pooling all ten queries instead gives a target pass proportion of 0.1.[2]

Neither average is an arithmetic error. Equal task weighting and equal query weighting answer different questions. Our reporting recommendation is to accompany aggregates with task counts, query counts per task and weights, and to establish the unit being averaged before comparing results. The synthetic 0.5 and 0.1 cannot be used to estimate the weighting effect on the paper's actual results.

## Numeric acceptance still needs domain bounds

The internal experiment includes a single-metric zero-error output. Although it meets the stipulated upper-bound error target, it fails the disclosed positive-value filter. Conversely, if the task contract permits another positive auxiliary metric, an error of −0.5 together with an auxiliary value of 1 can pass the numeric filter and a lower-is-better target. Controls include a normal error of 0.2 that passes, an error of 2 that misses its target, and sentinel, nonfinite and numeric-string cases that are rejected or receive no score.[2]

A negative root-mean-square error has no domain meaning. This construction separates generic properties such as finite, parseable and partly positive from the mathematical or physical range of an individual metric. An additional nonnegativity check in a real task would reject the negative-error case. We have no verified task implementation establishing whether that check exists, and the positive auxiliary metric is stipulated by the experimental contract. This is not evidence that the benchmark accepted such an actual output.

Likewise, extracting a number from result text does not itself bind that number to an executed analysis. We identify metric validity and execution provenance as evidence needed for interpretation; we do not claim that the original system necessarily lacks those mechanisms. Better coverage can be useful, and these cases do not refute the gains reported by the authors.

## Missing references leave two readings of reward

Separate from held-out scoring, the paper describes training-reward weights of 0.10 for sanity, 0.40 for target attainment and 0.50 for reference proximity. The authors report missing single-agent references for about 7% of queries and specify fallback weights of 0.40 and 0.60 for the remaining components, while describing renormalization.[1, Appendix C.3]

Proportional normalization of the surviving 0.10 and 0.40 would instead give 0.20 and 0.80. The internal experiment evaluates both the stated 0.40/0.60 values and that proportional reading. For a constructed input with sanity 1 and target pass 0, they yield rewards of 0.4 and 0.2, a difference of 0.2. When both components pass, both readings yield 1.[2]

The implemented weights and whether the authors intended to change them remain unknown. This ambiguity concerns training reward. It should not be merged with the held-out task score above or described as an observed leaderboard error.

## What still needs inspectable records

For users of scientific-agent evaluations, the supported action is specific: request task and query denominators, coverage, conditional target pass, valid metric ranges and executor-to-result provenance alongside the aggregate, while documenting training reward separately. Those records make it possible to distinguish producing more scoreable outputs from meeting targets more often on covered queries.

Assessing this benchmark's actual behavior requires the implemented fallback weights, task- and query-level records, metric bounds and execution-to-result traces. Raw outputs and equal-total-compute controls were also unavailable to this experiment. A bounded equation exercise can identify the questions to ask; it cannot answer for those missing records or establish a general benefit from multi-agent coordination.

## Research question

What machine-observable state should a long-running tool expose while it is still executing, and how much decision value can that state provide to a runtime scheduler without changing the agent's semantic observation? More importantly, where must the system stop treating progress telemetry as operational advice and refuse to promote it into business completion, retry authorization or effect truth?

## Signal taxonomy and mechanism

The paper's central distinction is between **predicting a tool's duration from outside** and **reading progress from the running call itself**. Historical estimates, per-tool distributions and learned predictors are fixed or derived from earlier calls; they can become stale when machine load, sandbox state, caches or the actual workload differ. The proposed progress stream is revised during the call.

Two signal strengths are distinguished. A **strong signal** says how much work remains, such as completed units out of a total. A **weak signal** says only that the end is near, for example completion of the last observable phase. Across the sampled corpora, after instrumentation strong signals cover substantial but incomplete portions of long tool time, and adding weak signals increases that coverage further. The paper therefore treats observability as graded rather than binary.

Signals can come from four places: tool output, an existing verbose/progress switch or dry run, an explicit hook/protocol, or code generated by the agent itself. The paper notes that explicit protocol hooks are the cleanest form but were rare in the sampled calls. It specifically identifies Model Context Protocol progress notifications as an existing mechanism whose missing piece is a serving-side consumer.

## Semantic isolation and event shape

The harness preserves the result path that the agent sees and sends progress through a second path to the serving system. Before execution, the call is registered under a call id; during execution an executor-side tap reads either the byte stream or a side copy, plus state left in the execution environment. Progress events share a common shape: **call id, work done, total if known, phase and timestamp**.

The wrapper has an output-reading layer and an environment-reading layer. The latter can infer progress from artifacts such as object files appearing, package-size metadata or test collection counts. The paper reports less than 1% wall-clock overhead for the combined stream in a realistic call. It also includes defensive behavior: if a counter exceeds its declared total, the wrapper retracts the percentage estimate rather than clamping to 100% and emitting a false completion signal.

The strongest semantic claim is deliberately narrow: the progress lines themselves do not enter the agent's observation, and the authors compare original versus instrumented outputs/exit status plus benchmark behavior. This supports separation of operational telemetry from the agent-visible semantic context for the tested instrumentation, but not a universal claim that every instrumentation strategy is behavior-preserving.

## Accuracy evidence

At the midpoint of a long call, the reported progress stream's median remaining-time error is about one fifth of call duration, versus about one third for the best history-based predictor under a favorable matching-history setup. Near 90% of a call's duration, the progress stream's median error is under 10%, while the best history predictor is around 80% in the paper's reported comparison.

History-based estimates are environment-sensitive. Replaying the same calls under idle and several contention patterns shows that matched history can remain useful, but pooled history can become roughly an order of magnitude worse near the end of a call. The live progress stream adapts after observing the new execution rate because it follows the current call instead of assuming the old environment still applies.

Across the SWE-bench Verified harness comparison, the side channel did not produce a statistically significant change in solved tasks, steps, completion tokens, tool time or wall time in the reported sample. More intrusive de-suppression altered many exit-code outcomes and did not improve useful signal share, which is important negative evidence: increasing observability by changing what the agent/tool sees can damage execution semantics.

## Serving-system evidence

The authors add a small set of hints to a vLLM-based serving engine and replay agent sessions on a 4 x H100 SXM machine in two memory configurations: GPU/HBM only and GPU plus host DRAM. With HBM only, the progress signal informs which idle KV context to retain or evict. With a host tier it also informs when to offload and when to prefetch a context back before the tool returns.

Against LRU, the paper reports **20.7% lower p90 post-tool-call TTFT in the HBM-only setting and 20.8% lower p90 in HBM + DRAM**. The text also summarizes the host-tier p90 reduction as about 21%, close to a 23% oracle, with much less DRAM reload traffic than prediction-based alternatives. Benefits rise with longer tool calls: the mean post-tool TTFT reduction grows to roughly one third for calls of at least ten seconds in the reported replay.

The residual gap to the oracle is primarily a coverage problem. A phase-level replay indicates that supplying truth where the harness currently hears nothing recovers much more of the oracle's remaining benefit than improving already-covered phases. This supports a practical engineering priority: increase explicit progress coverage before building ever more elaborate duration predictors.

## Untrusted telemetry and adversarial behavior

The paper explicitly refuses to give a progress report authority on its own because tenant-controlled code can lie. A script may report 90% completion before doing its expensive work, and the serving layer cannot infer that deception from the transcript before return.

To bound the damage, the proposed scheduler uses session credit. A session earns credit when reports prove accurate, spends credit when the engine acts on a report, and is charged when the eventual return shows the report was wrong. Separate ledgers bound memory and latency exposure. Once credit is exhausted, the session falls back to ordinary no-signal scheduling rather than gaining extra authority.

This mechanism is especially important for Runtime interpretation: **progress can be actionable without being authoritative**. The action it may influence is a bounded resource-scheduling choice; it does not certify that the tool has produced the requested business result.

## Claim-to-evidence map

1. **Live tool progress can outperform pre-call or history-based duration guesses at scheduling decision points.** Supported by midpoint, near-return and changing-environment comparisons.
2. **Operational progress can be exported without adding it to the agent-visible semantic context.** Supported by the separate state path, byte-preserving result path and harness comparison.
3. **Progress should be represented as a call-scoped event stream, not as one guessed completion time.** Supported by call-id keyed counts/totals/phases/timestamps and multi-phase handling.
4. **Explicit progress materially improves KV-cache scheduling in the evaluated serving system.** Supported by the HBM-only and HBM+DRAM end-to-end replay results.
5. **Coverage, not just estimator precision, limits the current benefit.** Supported by the phase-level oracle replay.
6. **Tool-originated telemetry must be treated as untrusted input.** Supported directly by the lying-reporter analysis and credit mechanism.

## Failure cases and limitations

Some calls reveal no useful state while running: a remote API that responds only at the end, a human-waiting command, or a download stalled before its first byte can be opaque. A runtime must therefore preserve an explicit **unknown progress** state rather than synthesize a percentage because it wants one.

Some formats cannot be parsed until a specific parser or explicit hook exists. In addition, revealing progress may itself change execution. Verbose flags, dry runs and instrumented scripts are operational modifications even when the agent's output bytes are unchanged. The paper measured the instruments it used, but every new integration needs its own equivalence evidence.

The serving evaluation concerns KV-cache residency, post-tool TTFT and throughput, not workflow correctness. A tool can reach 99% reported work and then fail, hang, roll back, produce the wrong artifact or commit an unintended external effect. The progress channel does not prove semantic success, task completion, authorization continuity, safe retry, idempotency or exactly-once behavior.

The reported credit mechanism bounds scheduling abuse; it is not an adversarial security proof for arbitrary tenants. The paper itself labels the lying-reporter result preliminary.

## Bounded Runtime finding

**Progress telemetry belongs to the execution-observability plane, not the business-truth plane.** A long-running Agent Runtime should keep at least these facts separate:

- **Execution identity:** which exact tool call is running;
- **Progress observation:** call-scoped work-done/total/phase/timestamp evidence, including `unknown` when unavailable;
- **Telemetry confidence:** whether the source is explicit, inferred, stale, malformed or historically unreliable;
- **Scheduling authority:** the bounded operational choices telemetry may influence, such as lease attention, cache residency or prefetch timing;
- **Business completion:** whether the requested result has actually reached its governed terminal condition;
- **Effect authorization and verification:** whether a state-changing action was permitted and whether the authoritative target state confirms the intended effect.

The paper gives strong evidence for using the first three layers to improve the fourth. It gives no basis for allowing progress percentage, near-completion or predicted return time to create the fifth or sixth.

For recovery logic, this implies a useful rule: live progress may justify **waiting longer or reallocating resources**, but it should not by itself authorize a duplicate retry. Retry still requires the repository's own lease/terminal/effect semantics, especially when the unknown side effect of the original call matters.

## Open questions

- Which tool protocols should expose standardized call-id, phase, completed-unit and total-unit fields natively rather than through parsers?
- How should a Runtime combine progress freshness with worker leases without allowing a lying or stuck tool to extend execution forever?
- What confidence downgrade should occur when instrumentation changes after a tool/version upgrade?
- Can progress telemetry be persisted as audit evidence without contaminating the semantic context supplied to the model?
- For external side-effecting tools, what postcondition/effect probe must accompany progress telemetry so recovery never confuses “nearly finished” with “safe to retry”?
